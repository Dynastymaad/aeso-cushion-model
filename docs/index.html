What the numbers mean, and how accurate they are

Reading a row

P50 is the middle outcome — as likely to settle above as below. It replaces the old "Likely", which was an average and therefore sat well above the typical hour on anything tight. P25 to P75 is the middle half of outcomes. P90 is the level exceeded one hour in ten. The three probability columns are the chance the hour settles above $50, $100 and $300.

P50 vs fair value (EV). P50 is the middle outcome — the better guess at what you will actually see. Fair value is the average outcome, which is the right number to price a contract off, because that is what it pays over many repetitions. On a quiet hour they sit within a few dollars. On a tight hour fair value can be double or triple P50, and that gap is the option value in the hour — most likely it is a P50 hour, but you are carrying something that occasionally prints $900.

On a tight hour the band is wide on purpose. If P25 reads $54 and P90 reads $994, that is the honest statement: this hour could be anything. Narrow bands on tight hours would be a lie.

How accurate they are

Every figure in this section is re-measured on each refresh, so it describes the model you are reading now rather than the one that shipped. Every hour's forecast used the wind, solar and load forecasts that were actually published the day before — never the outturn.

What the model still doesn't know

Wind, solar and load are all properly forecast. Gas availability and intertie flows still use the values that actually occurred, because no forecast archive exists for them. Together they are worth about 90 MW of cushion error against the 357 MW the model already carries, so the effect is small — but the real-world numbers will be slightly worse than the backtest.

Gas availability itself is pulled from AESO, not calculated. AESO publishes max capacity, mothballed capacity and outages by fuel type for the next 15 days, and available capacity is their subtraction, not ours. It reflects filed outage plans — so it is accurate about planned work and blind to a unit tripping. That is why availability barely moves hour to hour and why an unexpected trip is the scenario worth stress-testing with the sliders.

The $700 band is computed but not shown. It rests on 37 events in the test window — enough to suggest a number, not enough to stand behind one.

How it is built

Price comes from a curve fitted to the last seven days, because the relationship between spare capacity and price drifts constantly — the same 1,000 MW cushion meant a 34% chance of a spike in one quarter and 1% in another. The spread around that curve comes from a year of history, since a tail probability needs far more data than an average does. Both are split by time of day: the same cushion is meaningfully more dangerous in the evening, when solar falls away while load is still climbing.

Because tomorrow's cushion is itself uncertain by about 357 MW, the model does not read one point off the curve. It considers the whole range of cushions the forecast might really mean and blends the answers. That is what makes the bands honest rather than falsely precise.

What changed on 18 September

Refreshing on the 17 September event exposed two defects and one fragility. All three are fixed, and the accuracy figures above are measured on the fixed model.

The probability ladder could invert. The recalibration step was clipping flat once a reading ran past anything it had been trained on, and the three thresholds clipped at different heights — so on the most extreme hours the model could state a higher chance of clearing $300 than of clearing $100. It did that on 43% of scored hours, mildly on most of them. Thresholds are now forced into order and the recalibration extends rather than clips.

The model could state certainty it had not earned. The top of the recalibration curve rested on six training hours that all spiked, which is how a 100% came out of it. Thin bands are now merged until each holds at least twenty hours, each band is read as (hits + ½) ⁄ (n + 1), and nothing is ever stated above 95%. As a result the ladder has visible plateaus at the top — that is the evidence running out, shown rather than hidden.

The level curve is now trimmed. A seven-day window holds about eight hours in each cushion band, so one $716 hour was moving the whole tight end of the curve. The single highest hour in each band is trimmed before averaging. The tail did not go anywhere — it lives in the year of residual history, which is where there is enough data to shape it. Measured across 5,289 hours this cut the error by $0.91 an hour and halved the model's tendency to run hot after a spike week.

That tendency is real and worth knowing: in a week whose previous seven days held three or more hours above $300, the model's expected value still runs about $3.60 an hour high. In a quiet week it is within $0.62. Fade the model slightly in the week after a spike.
